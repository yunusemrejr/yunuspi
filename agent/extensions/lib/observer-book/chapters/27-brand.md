# Brand and identity

A brand is the set of associations people carry about a product. Design and words create those associations through repetition: the same promise, voice, colors, shapes and quality at every touchpoint. Identity work is less about a clever logo than about consistent, distinctive signals over time.

## Start with the promise, not the logo {#platform}
<!-- terms: brand promise mission values positioning platform purpose personality audience -->

**Principle.** Define what the brand promises, to whom, and with what personality before designing visual assets.

**Why.** Visual identity expresses a strategy; without one, it is decoration chosen by taste. A brand platform—audience, promise, proof points, personality traits, what the brand is not—gives every later decision (colors, typography, copy tone, imagery) a test. It also keeps work consistent across designers and years.

**Signals.** Logo or palette work with no stated audience or promise; brand discussions dominated by personal preference.

**Ask.** What does this brand promise and to whom, and does this design choice express it?

**Traps.** Lofty mission statements with no connection to what customers experience.

## Distinctive assets beat pretty ones {#distinctive-assets}
<!-- terms: distinctive assets recognizable memorable color shape mascot jingle unique differentiation category -->

**Principle.** Build a few ownable, distinctive assets—a color, a shape, a character, a sound, a phrase—and use them consistently for years.

**Why.** Recognition comes from distinctiveness plus repetition. Assets that look like the category norm (the same blue, the same geometric sans, the same gradient) cannot be linked to one brand. Research on brand growth shows that consistently used distinctive assets help people recognize and recall a brand in buying moments. Changing them often throws away built-up recognition.

**Signals.** Visual identity indistinguishable from competitors; frequent rebrands; assets used inconsistently across channels.

**Ask.** If the logo were removed, would people still recognize this as this brand?

**Traps.** Distinctiveness that sacrifices legibility or accessibility.

## Voice is part of the identity {#voice}
<!-- terms: voice tone personality copy wording words friendly formal playful consistent style -->

**Principle.** Define a consistent voice (the brand's personality in words) and adapt tone to the situation—playful in onboarding, calm and clear in errors.

**Why.** Words are touched far more often than logos: buttons, emails, errors, documentation. A consistent voice makes the product feel like one coherent entity. Tone must flex: humor in a payment failure message feels careless. Simple voice guidelines with do and don't examples keep many writers consistent.

**Signals.** Copy that shifts from formal to slangy between screens; jokes in error states; generic corporate phrasing.

**Ask.** Does this copy sound like the same entity as the rest of the product, with a tone fitting this moment?

**Traps.** Forced quirkiness that becomes grating on repetition.

## Logos must work small, mono and everywhere {#logo}
<!-- terms: logo logomark wordmark favicon app icon monochrome small size scalable svg lockup clear space -->

**Principle.** A logo must remain recognizable at favicon size, in one color, on light and dark backgrounds, with defined clear space and approved lockups.

**Why.** Logos live in many contexts: browser tabs, app icons, embroidery, dark mode headers, partner pages. Designs relying on fine detail, gradients or thin strokes fail small and in single color. SVG masters, simplified small-size variants and usage rules prevent distorted, recolored or crowded logos.

**Signals.** Logos with detail that disappears at small sizes; only a full-color version; stretched or recolored logos in the product.

**Ask.** Does the logo hold up at 16 pixels, in one color, on a dark background?

**Traps.** Over-designing the logo while neglecting the rest of the identity.

## Consistency across touchpoints builds trust {#touchpoints}
<!-- terms: touchpoints consistency email social website app docs packaging style guide templates -->

**Principle.** Apply the same identity system to every touchpoint—product, website, docs, email, social, sales material—through shared templates and tokens.

**Why.** Inconsistency signals disorganization and erodes trust, especially for products handling money or data. Users notice when the docs look unrelated to the app or emails come from a different visual world. Templates and design tokens make consistency cheap for everyone who publishes.

**Signals.** Documentation, emails or social images using different fonts, colors or logos than the product.

**Ask.** Would a customer recognize these touchpoints as the same brand side by side?

**Traps.** Rigid guidelines that prevent appropriate adaptation per channel.

## The design system is the brand at scale {#system}
<!-- terms: design system tokens components brand guidelines figma library governance -->

**Principle.** Encode brand decisions into the design system—tokens, components, imagery rules, voice examples—so the brand survives growth and turnover.

**Why.** Guidelines in a PDF are read once; tokens and components are used every day. When the brand lives in the system, every new page inherits it automatically. Governance (who can add variants, how changes propagate) keeps the system coherent, and a changelog for the system tells every team what moved.

**Signals.** Brand values hardcoded in pages; new components designed outside the system; no owner for brand decisions.

**Ask.** Where is this brand decision encoded so the next page gets it by default?

**Traps.** A system so rigid teams route around it.
