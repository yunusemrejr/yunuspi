# Color theory

Color is the most emotional and the most error-prone design tool. It carries brand, meaning and mood, but it is perceived non-linearly, varies across displays and cultures, and fails for a significant share of users with color vision deficiencies. Systematic color is designed in perceptual terms and expressed as roles, not raw values.

## Think in perceptual color spaces {#perceptual}
<!-- terms: oklch oklab lab hsl perceptual lightness uniform color space gamut p3 -->

**Principle.** Design palettes in a perceptual space such as OKLCH, where equal lightness steps look equal across hues; HSL lightness lies.

**Why.** In HSL, yellow and blue at the same "lightness" look dramatically different in brightness, so palettes built by rotating hue produce uneven contrast and muddy steps. OKLCH separates perceived lightness, chroma and hue, so a scale from 95% to 20% lightness produces evenly spaced shades for every hue, and contrast becomes predictable. Modern CSS supports oklch() directly, with wide-gamut (P3) colors where displays allow.

**Signals.** Palettes generated by adjusting HSL lightness; shades of different hues with inconsistent contrast; muddy mid-tones.

**Ask.** Were these shades built on perceived lightness, so each step has predictable contrast across hues?

**Traps.** Out-of-gamut colors silently clipped; chasing wide gamut where brand colors must match print.

## Choose a harmony, then restrain it {#harmony}
<!-- terms: harmony complementary analogous triadic split complementary monochromatic scheme accent -->

**Principle.** Start from a harmony scheme—monochromatic, analogous, complementary, split-complementary, triadic—then let one hue lead and use the others sparingly.

**Why.** Harmony schemes are relationships on the color wheel that reliably look intentional. Monochromatic and analogous schemes feel calm and cohesive; complementary schemes create vibrancy and strong accents; triadic schemes are lively but hard to balance. The common failure is using all scheme colors at equal strength. One dominant hue with a restrained supporting hue and a single accent reads as designed.

**Signals.** Several saturated hues competing; accent color used for large areas; palettes without a clear dominant hue.

**Ask.** Which hue leads this palette, and is the accent reserved for what deserves attention?

**Traps.** Complementary pairs at full saturation side by side (vibrating edges).

## Distribute color 60-30-10 {#sixty-thirty-ten}
<!-- terms: 60-30-10 distribution proportion dominant secondary accent neutral background surface -->

**Principle.** Let roughly 60% of the surface be a dominant neutral or base, 30% a secondary color, and 10% an accent that marks action and emphasis.

**Why.** The proportion rule, borrowed from interior design, prevents both monotony and chaos. In interfaces the 60% is usually neutrals (backgrounds, surfaces), the 30% structural color (navigation, headers, secondary surfaces), and the 10% the brand or action color (primary buttons, links, highlights). An accent only works while it is rare; spread widely it loses its signal.

**Signals.** Brand color used for backgrounds, text, borders and buttons alike; no neutral base; accents everywhere.

**Ask.** What share of this screen does the accent color occupy, and does it still point to the primary action?

**Traps.** Applying the ratio mechanically to data visualizations or illustrations.

## Name colors by role, not by value {#semantic-tokens}
<!-- terms: semantic tokens role primary surface background foreground muted border danger success warning variables custom properties -->

**Principle.** Define semantic tokens—background, surface, text, muted text, border, primary, danger, success, warning, focus—mapped to palette values, and use only the tokens in components.

**Why.** Components that reference raw hex values cannot be re-themed, cannot support dark mode, and drift as designers tweak shades. Semantic tokens separate meaning from appearance: "danger" stays danger while its value changes per theme. They also make contrast auditable per role pair (text on surface, primary text on primary background).

**Signals.** Hex values scattered in component styles; the same color named differently in places; dark mode implemented by overriding individual components.

**Ask.** Does every color in this component come from a semantic token, and does each token have a defined contrast partner?

**Traps.** Token explosions with dozens of near-duplicate roles.

## Contrast is a requirement, not a preference {#contrast}
<!-- terms: contrast ratio wcag 4.5 3:1 aa aaa readable legible gray text low contrast apca -->

**Principle.** Body text needs at least 4.5:1 contrast against its background, large text and interface components at least 3:1—checked for every state.

**Why.** Light gray text on white is fashionable and unreadable for many users, in sunlight, on cheap displays and with aging eyes. WCAG ratios are the legal and practical baseline; newer models like APCA account for font weight and polarity. Contrast must be checked for hover, focus, disabled, placeholder and error states, and over images and gradients, where it varies across the element.

**Signals.** Muted text in light grays; text over photos without overlays; placeholder text as the only instruction; focus rings nearly invisible.

**Ask.** Has every text and control color pair here been checked for contrast, including states and text over images?

**Traps.** Maximum contrast everywhere causing glare; relying on a single automated check for complex backgrounds.

## Never use color alone to carry meaning {#color-blind}
<!-- terms: color blind colorblind deficiency red green status icon pattern redundant encoding -->

**Principle.** Pair color with a second cue—icon, text, shape, pattern or position—for status, errors, selections and data series.

**Why.** Around 8% of men have some color vision deficiency, most commonly red-green. Red for error and green for success are indistinguishable for them, as are many chart palettes. Redundant encoding (an icon plus color, a label plus color, dashed plus colored lines) makes meaning robust for everyone, including in grayscale printouts and bright sunlight.

**Signals.** Status shown only by colored dots; charts distinguished only by hue; form errors marked only by red borders.

**Ask.** If this screen were grayscale, would every status and series still be distinguishable?

**Traps.** Adding redundant cues that clutter the interface when text already carries the meaning.

## Dark mode is its own design {#dark-mode}
<!-- terms: dark mode dark theme light theme inversion elevation surface shadow saturation -->

**Principle.** Build dark themes from dark grays with lighter surfaces for elevation, desaturated accents and re-checked contrast—not by inverting the light palette.

**Why.** Pure black backgrounds with pure white text cause halation and eye strain; saturated colors vibrate on dark backgrounds; shadows disappear, so elevation must be expressed by lighter surfaces. Images and illustrations may need dimming or alternate versions. Contrast ratios change in both directions, so every token pair must be re-verified.

**Signals.** Dark mode implemented with filter: invert or naive swaps; saturated brand colors unchanged on dark backgrounds; shadows used for elevation in dark theme.

**Ask.** Was the dark theme designed with its own surfaces and accent values, and re-checked for contrast?

**Traps.** Forgetting charts, code blocks, embedded content and third-party widgets in dark mode.

## Color carries psychology and culture {#psychology}
<!-- terms: psychology emotion meaning culture brand trust warm cool red blue green yellow luxury energy calm -->

**Principle.** Choose colors for the associations they carry with this audience and category, and test assumptions rather than relying on universal "color meanings".

**Why.** Warm colors read as energetic, urgent or appetizing; cool colors as calm, trustworthy or technical; desaturated and dark palettes as premium; bright saturated palettes as playful. But meanings are cultural (white for weddings or mourning, red for luck or danger) and category-specific (finance leans blue for trust; food uses warm tones). Differentiation also matters: a category full of blue brands rewards a distinct hue.

**Signals.** Palette choices justified only by personal taste; colors that contradict the product's positioning; category-typical colors that make the brand invisible.

**Ask.** What should this palette make the target audience feel, and does it distinguish the product from its competitors?

**Traps.** Pop-psychology claims presented as fact; sacrificing accessibility for mood.

## Gradients and palettes for data need discipline {#data-palettes}
<!-- terms: gradient sequential diverging categorical palette chart data visualization heatmap colormap viridis -->

**Principle.** Use sequential palettes for ordered values, diverging palettes around a meaningful midpoint, and categorical palettes with distinct, equally prominent hues; interpolate gradients in a perceptual space.

**Why.** Rainbow colormaps create false boundaries and mislead magnitude judgments; perceptually uniform maps (viridis-like) represent values honestly and survive color blindness. Categorical palettes need hues distinct enough to tell apart but similar in lightness, so no series dominates by accident. Gradients interpolated in sRGB pass through gray "dead zones"; OKLCH or OKLAB interpolation keeps them vivid.

**Signals.** Rainbow color scales; categorical palettes with one much brighter color; gradients with muddy midpoints.

**Ask.** Does this palette's structure match the data's structure (ordered, diverging or categorical)?

**Traps.** Too many categories for color to distinguish; decorative gradients reducing text contrast.
