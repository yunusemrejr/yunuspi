# Copywriting

Copy is the interface between a product's value and a reader's attention. Good copy is clear first, specific second, and persuasive third—persuasion without clarity is noise. Most bad copy fails by being vague, self-centered or generic.

## Clarity beats cleverness {#clarity}
<!-- terms: clarity clear simple plain language clever pun wordplay jargon understand -->

**Principle.** Say exactly what the product does and for whom in plain words; clever lines that need decoding lose readers.

**Why.** Readers skim and decide in seconds. A clever headline that requires interpretation fails the skim test, while a clear one ("Invoices paid twice as fast") communicates instantly. Jargon and buzzwords ("synergistic AI-powered platform") signal nothing. Clarity also tests the product's positioning: if the value cannot be said plainly, it may not be clear yet.

**Signals.** Headlines that do not say what the product is; buzzwords; metaphors requiring explanation.

**Ask.** Would a stranger understand what this is and who it is for after reading the headline once?

**Traps.** Clarity so bland it could describe any product.

## Specific beats general {#specificity}
<!-- terms: specific specificity concrete numbers details example precise vague generic claim -->

**Principle.** Replace general claims with specific ones: numbers, named situations, concrete outcomes and vivid examples.

**Why.** "Save time" is ignored; "Close your books in 2 days instead of 10" is believed and remembered. Specificity signals honesty (vague claims feel evasive) and helps readers see themselves in the scenario. Concrete details also differentiate, because generic claims are shared by every competitor.

**Signals.** Words like "easily", "powerful", "seamless", "best-in-class"; benefits without numbers or examples.

**Ask.** Which generic phrase here could be replaced with a specific number, situation or example?

**Traps.** Precise-sounding numbers without evidence.

## Lead with benefits, support with features {#benefits}
<!-- terms: benefit benefits feature features outcome value so that means why care -->

**Principle.** Open with what the reader gains, then show the feature that delivers it—connecting them with "so you can".

**Why.** Readers care about their outcomes, not the product's mechanisms. Features provide credibility and detail for those evaluating seriously. The "so what?" test turns features into benefits: "Offline sync" becomes "Keep working on the plane—changes sync when you land". Technical audiences still want features, but framed by the problem they solve.

**Signals.** Feature lists without outcomes; copy centered on "we" and "our" rather than "you".

**Ask.** For each feature mentioned, what does the reader get from it?

**Traps.** Benefit claims disconnected from any feature that delivers them.

## Use proven structures {#frameworks}
<!-- terms: aida attention interest desire action pas problem agitate solution bab before after bridge 4u formula structure -->

**Principle.** Structure persuasive copy with proven frameworks—Problem-Agitate-Solution, Before-After-Bridge, AIDA—adapted to the audience's awareness.

**Why.** Frameworks encode how persuasion unfolds: recognize a problem, feel its cost, see a solution, get proof, take action. PAS works for problem-aware readers; Before-After-Bridge paints the transformation; AIDA sequences a whole page. They prevent common structural failures such as jumping to features before establishing the problem.

**Signals.** Pages that start with product features; no problem statement; calls to action before any proof.

**Ask.** What structure does this copy follow, and does it match what the reader already knows?

**Traps.** Formulaic copy that sounds like every other landing page.

## Headlines carry most of the weight {#headlines}
<!-- terms: headline headlines title subject line hook promise curiosity useful urgent unique specific -->

**Principle.** Write many headline candidates and choose the one that is most useful, specific and relevant to the target reader; the subheadline explains how.

**Why.** Most readers read only the headline. Good headlines promise a specific outcome to a specific audience (the "4 U's": useful, urgent, unique, ultra-specific). Writing ten or twenty options surfaces better angles than polishing the first. Testing headlines (when traffic allows) settles debates with data.

**Signals.** One headline considered; headlines about the company rather than the reader; vague superlatives.

**Ask.** Which of several headline options makes the most specific, relevant promise to the target reader?

**Traps.** Clickbait headlines that the content does not deliver.

## Calls to action say what happens next {#cta}
<!-- terms: cta call to action button text sign up get started try free start trial download next step friction -->

**Principle.** Button and link text should describe the action and its value ("Start free trial", "Get the template"), with one primary call to action per page section and reassurance near it.

**Why.** Generic calls to action ("Submit", "Click here") give no reason to act. Specific verbs and outcomes set expectations. Friction-reducing microcopy nearby ("No credit card required", "Cancel anytime") answers the last objection at the moment of decision. Too many competing calls to action split attention.

**Signals.** Buttons labelled Submit or Learn more everywhere; several primary actions competing; no reassurance near sign-up.

**Ask.** Does this button tell the reader exactly what they get when they click?

**Traps.** Reassurances that are not true.

## Microcopy guides and reassures {#microcopy}
<!-- terms: microcopy ux writing error message empty state label tooltip confirmation success message onboarding -->

**Principle.** Write interface text—labels, errors, empty states, confirmations—to guide the next action in the user's language, briefly and kindly.

**Why.** Microcopy is where users feel the product's personality and where confusion costs most. Errors should say what happened and how to fix it without blame; empty states should suggest the first action; confirmations for destructive actions should name exactly what will happen. Consistent terminology across the interface prevents doubt.

**Signals.** Error messages with codes or blame; empty states that say only "No data"; inconsistent names for the same concept.

**Ask.** Does each piece of interface text tell users what happened and what to do next?

**Traps.** Humor in error messages at moments of frustration.

## Edit ruthlessly and sound human {#editing}
<!-- terms: edit editing cut shorten concise wordy filler generic ai sounding cliches voice human natural -->

**Principle.** Cut every word that does not earn its place, replace clichés with concrete language, and read the copy aloud to catch unnatural phrasing.

**Why.** First drafts are long; good copy is short. Filler ("in order to", "very", "really"), hedges and clichés dilute meaning. Generic AI-sounding prose—stacked adjectives, "unlock", "elevate", "seamless", "in today's fast-paced world"—makes readers disengage instantly. Reading aloud reveals rhythm problems and sentences no human would say.

**Signals.** Long paragraphs on landing pages; clichés and generic buzzwords; sentences that sound unnatural aloud.

**Ask.** Which words could be cut without losing meaning, and which phrases sound like no person would say them?

**Traps.** Cutting so much that necessary context disappears.
