# Marketing strategy and positioning

Marketing is the work of making the right people understand why a product matters to them. Tactics—ads, posts, emails, launches—amplify a message; they cannot create one. Strategy decides who the product is for, what it is compared against, and why it wins for those people.

## Position against the real alternative {#positioning}
<!-- terms: positioning competitive alternative context category unique value for whom instead of -->

**Principle.** Define positioning as: for [specific customer] who [has this problem], unlike [the real alternative they use today], we [unique capability] so they [meaningful outcome].

**Why.** Customers understand products by comparison. The real alternative is often not a competitor but a spreadsheet, an agency, a manual process or doing nothing. Naming it clarifies which unique capabilities matter and which value to emphasize. Positioning also picks the market category that makes the product's strengths obvious—being the best in a small, well-chosen frame beats being average in a large one.

**Signals.** Messaging that describes features without a comparison; target audience "everyone"; no clear category.

**Ask.** What would the target customer use if this product did not exist, and why is this better for them specifically?

**Traps.** Positioning against competitors customers have never heard of.

## Choose an ideal customer profile and say no to others {#icp}
<!-- terms: icp ideal customer profile segment target niche beachhead early adopters persona firmographic -->

**Principle.** Focus on a narrow segment that feels the problem most acutely, can buy, and can be reached efficiently—and design messaging and channels for them first.

**Why.** Messages written for everyone persuade no one. A beachhead segment with urgent pain and shared channels (the same communities, publications, events) allows word of mouth and efficient acquisition. Early adopters tolerate rough edges when the pain is acute. Expansion comes after winning the first segment convincingly.

**Signals.** Campaigns addressing many audiences at once; personas defined by demographics instead of problems and buying context.

**Ask.** Which specific segment feels this pain most and can be reached through shared channels?

**Traps.** Choosing a segment too small to sustain the business.

## Use the customer's own words {#voice-of-customer}
<!-- terms: voice of customer customer language reviews interviews quotes words phrases pain points jargon -->

**Principle.** Mine interviews, reviews, support tickets and community discussions for the exact words customers use about their problems, and write with those words.

**Why.** Customers search, skim and recognize their own language, not internal jargon. Phrases lifted from real customer descriptions ("I spend every Monday reconciling spreadsheets") resonate far more than polished abstractions ("streamlined financial operations"). Competitor reviews reveal unmet needs and objections.

**Signals.** Copy full of internal terminology; messaging never tested against customer language.

**Ask.** Would the target customer describe their problem in these words?

**Traps.** Copying complaints so literally that messaging turns negative.

## Build a message hierarchy {#message-hierarchy}
<!-- terms: message hierarchy headline value proposition pillars benefits features proof supporting points -->

**Principle.** Structure messaging as one core promise, three supporting pillars, and proof for each pillar; every asset draws from this hierarchy.

**Why.** Inconsistent messaging across the website, ads, sales decks and product confuses the market. A hierarchy keeps the core promise constant while allowing channel-specific expression. Pillars organize benefits; proof (numbers, demos, case studies) makes each pillar believable.

**Signals.** Different headlines and claims on every page; features listed without grouping or proof.

**Ask.** What is the one promise, what three pillars support it, and where is the proof for each?

**Traps.** Too many pillars diluting the message.

## Proof beats claims {#proof}
<!-- terms: proof social proof testimonial case study numbers data demo evidence credibility logos reviews -->

**Principle.** Replace adjectives with evidence: specific numbers with sources, named customer stories, live demos, benchmarks and transparent comparisons.

**Why.** Every product claims to be fast, easy and powerful; audiences discount unsupported adjectives to zero. Specific, verifiable proof ("cut invoice processing from 3 days to 4 hours at Acme, a 40-person agency") is credible and memorable. Demonstrations are the strongest proof because the audience sees for themselves. Proof must be real: fabricated metrics or testimonials destroy trust permanently and may be illegal.

**Signals.** Claims like "10x faster" without source; generic testimonials; logos of companies that are not customers.

**Ask.** What verifiable evidence supports this claim, and could the audience check it?

**Traps.** Cherry-picked benchmarks presented as typical.

## Meet the audience at their awareness stage {#awareness}
<!-- terms: awareness stage unaware problem aware solution aware product aware most aware funnel top middle bottom -->

**Principle.** Match the message to how much the audience already knows: problem education for the unaware, differentiation for the solution-aware, offers and reassurance for the product-aware.

**Why.** A detailed feature comparison bores people who do not yet recognize their problem; problem education annoys people ready to buy. Eugene Schwartz's awareness stages map messaging to readiness. Different channels reach different stages: search captures active intent, social and content reach earlier stages.

**Signals.** One message used across all channels; bottom-of-funnel offers shown to cold audiences.

**Ask.** What does this audience already know, and does the message start there?

**Traps.** Educational content that never connects to the product.

## Anticipate and answer objections {#objections}
<!-- terms: objection objections risk concern price trust switching cost guarantee faq reassurance -->

**Principle.** List the reasons a qualified buyer would hesitate—price, risk, effort to switch, trust, fit—and answer each with proof, guarantees or product changes.

**Why.** Most qualified prospects who do not convert have unanswered objections. FAQs, comparison pages, migration guides, free trials, guarantees and security documentation address them directly. Sales and support conversations are the best source of real objections.

**Signals.** No FAQ or comparison content; switching costs ignored; security or privacy questions unanswered on the site.

**Ask.** What stops a qualified buyer from saying yes today, and where is each objection answered?

**Traps.** Answering objections nobody has while ignoring real ones.

## Ethical persuasion only {#ethics}
<!-- terms: ethics dark patterns fake scarcity urgency manipulation honest transparent trust compliance -->

**Principle.** Persuade with true, relevant information; never use fake scarcity, fabricated social proof, hidden costs, or manipulative dark patterns.

**Why.** Manipulative tactics may lift short-term conversion but increase refunds, churn, complaints and regulatory risk, and they damage brands when exposed. Real urgency (a genuine deadline) and real social proof are effective and honest. Long-term growth depends on trust, especially for products that ask for data or payment.

**Signals.** Countdown timers that reset; invented user counts; pre-checked upsells; hard-to-find cancellation.

**Ask.** Would this tactic still work if the customer fully understood it?

**Traps.** Assuming competitors' dark patterns make them acceptable.
