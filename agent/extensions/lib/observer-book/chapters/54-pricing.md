# Pricing and business models

Price is the most powerful lever a product has and usually the least examined. It communicates positioning, determines who the customers are, and funds everything else. Pricing well means understanding the value delivered, choosing what to charge for, and packaging it so customers can choose easily.

## Price on value, not cost {#value-based}
<!-- terms: value based pricing willingness to pay cost plus competitor pricing economic value customer segments -->

**Principle.** Anchor prices to the economic value customers receive and their willingness to pay, using costs as a floor and competitors as context.

**Why.** Cost-plus pricing leaves money on the table when value is high and loses money when value is low. Customers compare price to value and to their alternatives. Different segments receive different value, which is why tiers exist. Research through interviews and willingness-to-pay studies (for example Van Westendorp surveys) grounds price in reality.

**Signals.** Prices set by copying a competitor or adding margin to cost; no research on customer willingness to pay.

**Ask.** What value does this deliver to the target customer, and how does the price compare to it?

**Traps.** Underpricing to win customers, then struggling to raise prices.

## Choose a value metric that grows with value {#value-metric}
<!-- terms: value metric per seat per user usage based consumption credits per project scaling expansion revenue -->

**Principle.** Charge along the dimension that grows as customers get more value—seats, usage, projects, revenue processed—so revenue expands with customer success.

**Why.** A good value metric feels fair, is easy to understand and predict, and scales with value delivered. Per-seat pricing suits collaboration tools; usage-based pricing suits infrastructure and APIs; outcome-based pricing aligns incentives but is harder to measure. A poor metric (charging for something customers do not associate with value) creates friction and churn.

**Signals.** Pricing that does not grow with usage or value; metrics customers cannot predict or control.

**Ask.** As a customer gets more value from the product, does what they pay grow in a way they find fair?

**Traps.** Usage pricing that makes bills unpredictable and scary.

## Package into clear tiers {#packaging}
<!-- terms: packaging tiers good better best plans features gating enterprise starter pro comparison table -->

**Principle.** Offer a small number of tiers aimed at distinct segments (for example starter, professional, enterprise), with differences customers understand at a glance.

**Why.** Too many options cause decision paralysis; too few leave segments underserved. Good-better-best packaging lets customers self-select, with the middle tier designed as the default choice. Feature gating should follow segment needs (advanced controls, security features and support for larger organizations), not arbitrary limits.

**Signals.** Many plans with overlapping features; comparison tables too long to read; limits that feel arbitrary.

**Ask.** Can a customer tell within seconds which plan is for them?

**Traps.** Gating the core value so heavily that free or entry users never experience it.

## Use anchoring and framing honestly {#psychology}
<!-- terms: anchoring anchor decoy effect framing charm pricing annual discount comparison psychology perception -->

**Principle.** Present prices so value is easy to judge—showing higher tiers first, annual savings and per-unit comparisons—without inventing fake reference prices or decoys meant to mislead.

**Why.** People judge prices relative to reference points. A visible enterprise tier makes the professional tier look reasonable; annual plans framed as savings increase commitment; per-day framing can clarify value. These effects are powerful and legitimate when the numbers are real. Fake "original prices", phantom discounts and decoys designed to confuse cross into deception and legal risk.

**Signals.** Strikethrough prices that were never charged; perpetual limited-time discounts; plans designed only as confusing decoys.

**Ask.** Would customers feel misled if they understood how these prices are framed?

**Traps.** Charm pricing ($x.99) on premium products where it signals cheapness.

## Free tiers and trials are acquisition channels {#free}
<!-- terms: free tier freemium free trial reverse trial conversion rate upgrade paywall limits activation -->

**Principle.** Design free tiers and trials around reaching value and hitting natural upgrade moments, and measure conversion, not just sign-ups.

**Why.** Freemium works when free users experience real value, spread the product and hit limits that correlate with willingness to pay. Trials work when users reach value within the trial period. Reverse trials (full features temporarily, then a free tier) combine both. Free tiers that are too generous remove reasons to pay; too stingy and they fail to demonstrate value.

**Signals.** Free tiers with no upgrade path; trials shorter than the time to value; success measured by sign-ups alone.

**Ask.** At what moment does a free user naturally need to pay, and how many reach it?

**Traps.** Supporting large free user bases whose costs exceed their marketing value.

## Know the unit economics {#unit-economics}
<!-- terms: unit economics ltv cac payback churn gross margin arpu mrr arr cohort retention revenue -->

**Principle.** Track customer acquisition cost, lifetime value, payback period, churn and gross margin by segment; growth with bad unit economics accelerates losses.

**Why.** A business is healthy when customers are worth more than they cost to acquire and serve. Lifetime value depends on churn and margin; payback period determines how much cash growth consumes. Averages hide segment differences: some channels or customer types may be deeply unprofitable. Usage-heavy customers can destroy margins under flat pricing.

**Signals.** Growth metrics without cost or margin data; flat pricing for customers with widely varying usage costs.

**Ask.** For this segment, how long does it take to recover acquisition cost, and what does serving them cost?

**Traps.** Optimistic lifetime values based on short histories.

## Test and communicate price changes carefully {#changes}
<!-- terms: price change increase grandfather migration test communicate announce existing customers -->

**Principle.** Test pricing with new customers or segments, grandfather or give notice to existing customers, and explain changes in terms of value delivered.

**Why.** Price changes affect revenue, churn and trust at once. Testing on new sign-ups limits risk; grandfathering or long notice periods respect existing customers; explaining what improved since the last price earns acceptance. Surprise increases with vague justifications cause backlash and churn.

**Signals.** Price increases applied to existing customers without notice; changes made without measuring impact.

**Ask.** How will existing customers learn about this change, and what value justifies it?

**Traps.** Never raising prices despite substantial added value.
