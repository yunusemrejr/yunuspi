# Product promotion and launches

A launch is a concentrated moment of attention. It works when there is something genuinely new, the assets make the value obvious in seconds, the right communities hear about it in their own norms, and the team is ready to convert attention into users and conversations. Most launches fail from weak assets and bad timing, not from lack of channels.

## Sequence the launch: warm up, peak, sustain {#sequence}
<!-- terms: launch plan sequence waitlist beta early access teaser warm up day one follow up momentum timeline -->

**Principle.** Build an audience before launch (waitlist, beta users, build-in-public updates), concentrate announcements into a coordinated peak, and plan follow-ups for the weeks after.

**Why.** Launching to no audience wastes the moment. Beta users provide testimonials, bug reports and day-one advocates; a waitlist provides a first wave. Coordinating posts, emails, partners and communities within a short window creates visible momentum that feeds platform algorithms and press interest. Most traffic decays within days, so follow-up content (case studies, deep dives, updates) extends the tail.

**Signals.** Launch planned as a single post; no audience or beta users before launch day; no content planned after.

**Ask.** Who already knows about this before launch day, and what happens in the two weeks after?

**Traps.** Endless pre-launch hype for a product that is not ready.

## Assets make the value obvious in five seconds {#assets}
<!-- terms: demo video gif screenshot hero image og image social card thumbnail tagline one-liner landing page -->

**Principle.** Prepare a sharp one-line description, a short demo video or GIF showing the core moment, clear screenshots, a social preview image and a landing page that converts the traffic.

**Why.** Launch audiences skim. A 30–60 second demo that shows the product solving a real problem outperforms any description. Social preview images (Open Graph cards) determine how links look when shared. The landing page must match the launch message and offer an obvious next step (try, sign up, star). Weak assets waste the attention the launch creates.

**Signals.** Launch posts with text only; generic social preview images; demo videos with slow intros; landing page messaging different from the launch post.

**Ask.** Could someone understand what this does and why it matters from the demo and one-liner alone?

**Traps.** Polished marketing videos that hide the actual product.

## Respect each platform's norms {#platforms}
<!-- terms: product hunt hacker news show hn reddit subreddit rules self promotion twitter linkedin norms community etiquette -->

**Principle.** Tailor the launch to each platform: Product Hunt rewards polished assets and engaged makers; Show HN rewards technical substance and honesty; Reddit requires genuine participation within each subreddit's rules.

**Why.** The same announcement copy-pasted everywhere reads as spam. Hacker News audiences respond to how something works, trade-offs and humility, and punish marketing language. Many subreddits ban self-promotion or allow it only from established members. Product Hunt launches benefit from preparation and engaging with every comment. Vote manipulation and fake accounts are detected and penalized, and they damage reputation.

**Signals.** Identical posts across platforms; marketing superlatives in technical communities; new accounts posting promotional content.

**Ask.** What does this specific community value, and does the post give them that?

**Traps.** Asking friends to upvote, which platforms treat as manipulation.

## Outreach is personal and relevant {#outreach}
<!-- terms: press journalist outreach pitch email creator influencer partnership podcast newsletter exclusive embargo -->

**Principle.** Pitch journalists, newsletter writers and creators individually with a story that fits their audience, concrete assets and a clear reason why now.

**Why.** Mass-blasted press releases are ignored. Writers need stories: a surprising insight, data, a trend, a customer transformation. Showing that you know their work and audience earns attention. Exclusives and embargoes can help with larger outlets. Niche newsletters and creators often convert better than mainstream press because their audiences trust them and match the target segment.

**Signals.** Generic press releases sent to large lists; pitches about features without a story; no assets attached.

**Ask.** Why would this writer's audience care about this story right now?

**Traps.** Paying for coverage without disclosure.

## Open-source projects promote through their repository {#open-source}
<!-- terms: open source github readme stars contributors documentation examples quickstart badges awesome list release notes -->

**Principle.** For open-source launches, the README is the landing page: a clear one-liner, a visual demo, a copy-paste quickstart, and honest status; then share where developers who need it gather.

**Why.** Developers decide in seconds from the README whether to try a project. A quickstart that works on the first try converts interest into usage; examples and docs convert usage into adoption. Stars follow usefulness, not the reverse. Awesome lists, relevant issue threads, technical blog posts and conference talks reach developers organically. Release notes and changelogs signal an active, trustworthy project.

**Signals.** READMEs that start with badges and installation minutiae; no demo or examples; quickstarts that fail.

**Ask.** Can a developer understand, install and see this project work within five minutes of reading the README?

**Traps.** Buying stars or using star-exchange schemes.

## Turn every release into content {#changelog-marketing}
<!-- terms: changelog release notes update announcement feature announcement shipping cadence newsletter build in public -->

**Principle.** Communicate releases regularly with user-focused changelogs, short demos of new features and updates to users who asked for them.

**Why.** A visible cadence of improvements signals a living product and gives reasons to return. Each release is content: a GIF of the new feature, a note to users who requested it, a post explaining the problem it solves. Building in public (sharing progress, metrics and lessons) builds an audience over time.

**Signals.** Features shipped silently; changelogs of internal jargon; users who requested features never told they shipped.

**Ask.** Who would care about this release, and how will they hear about it?

**Traps.** Announcing trivial changes so often that updates become noise.

## Convert attention into relationships {#convert}
<!-- terms: convert conversion follow up onboarding feedback response comments support signup capture email -->

**Principle.** During and after a launch, respond quickly to every comment and question, fix reported issues visibly, and capture interested people (sign-ups, newsletter, community) so the relationship outlasts the spike.

**Why.** Launch attention decays fast; the lasting value is the people who engaged. Responsive founders earn goodwill and word of mouth; ignored comments look like abandonment. Visible quick fixes during a launch are themselves strong marketing. Capturing contact details (with consent) turns visitors into an audience you can reach again.

**Signals.** Unanswered launch comments; bugs reported during launch left unacknowledged; no way to follow the project.

**Ask.** What happens to the people who show interest today, and how will you reach them next month?

**Traps.** Defensive responses to criticism in public threads.
