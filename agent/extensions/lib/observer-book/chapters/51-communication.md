# Communication and language

Communication succeeds when the recipient understands and can act, not when the sender has said everything. Most workplace communication fails the same ways: the point buried, the wrong level of detail for the audience, ambiguity where precision was needed, and tone that undermines the content.

## Bottom line up front {#bluf}
<!-- terms: bluf bottom line up front summary conclusion first executive summary key point tldr -->

**Principle.** State the conclusion, decision or request in the first sentence; follow with the supporting detail in descending importance.

**Why.** Readers are busy and often stop early. Messages that build up to the point waste their time and risk the point never being read. Leading with the bottom line lets readers decide how much detail they need. Requests should be explicit and near the top, with deadlines: "Please approve the budget by Friday" rather than a hint in the last paragraph.

**Signals.** Key decisions or requests buried at the end; long context before the point; summaries that recount chronology.

**Ask.** If the reader stopped after the first sentence, would they know the point and what is being asked?

**Traps.** Delivering bad news so abruptly that necessary context is missing.

## Write for the reader in front of you {#audience}
<!-- terms: audience reader register technical non-technical executive expert beginner level of detail jargon -->

**Principle.** Adjust vocabulary, detail and framing to the specific reader: executives need decisions and impact, engineers need specifics, users need steps and outcomes.

**Why.** The same content fails when pitched at the wrong level: jargon loses non-experts, over-explanation insults experts, technical detail buries the business impact for decision-makers. Naming the reader and their question before writing prevents mismatch. When writing for mixed audiences, layer the message: summary for everyone, details for those who need them.

**Signals.** Technical jargon in messages to non-technical stakeholders; impact missing from executive updates; condescending explanations to experts.

**Ask.** Who exactly is reading this, what do they already know, and what do they need to decide?

**Traps.** Oversimplifying until the message is wrong.

## Plain language is precise language {#plain-language}
<!-- terms: plain language simple words short sentences active voice concrete ambiguous vague precise -->

**Principle.** Use short sentences, common words, active voice and concrete nouns; avoid ambiguity where it could cause the wrong action.

**Why.** Plain language is faster to read, easier to translate and harder to misunderstand. Passive voice hides who does what ("the database will be migrated"—by whom?). Vague words ("soon", "some issues", "should be fine") hide commitments. Precision about who, what, when and how much prevents costly misunderstandings.

**Signals.** Long sentences with nested clauses; passive constructions hiding responsibility; vague time and quantity words.

**Ask.** Could anything in this message be interpreted in a way that leads to the wrong action?

**Traps.** Over-precision that makes simple messages legalistic.

## Structure reveals thinking {#structure}
<!-- terms: structure headings bullets lists paragraphs organize scan skim sections order pyramid principle -->

**Principle.** Organize messages so structure mirrors the logic: headings for sections, lists for parallel items, one idea per paragraph, and grouped arguments under their conclusion.

**Why.** Structured writing is scannable and makes relationships between ideas explicit. The pyramid principle—conclusion first, then grouped supporting arguments, each with evidence—makes complex arguments easy to follow and critique. Walls of text hide both good and bad reasoning.

**Signals.** Long unbroken paragraphs; lists mixing unrelated items; arguments without a stated conclusion.

**Ask.** Does the structure of this message make its logic visible at a glance?

**Traps.** Over-formatting short messages into slide-like fragments.

## Persuade with credibility, logic and meaning {#rhetoric}
<!-- terms: persuasion persuade rhetoric ethos pathos logos credibility emotion logic argument evidence convince -->

**Principle.** Persuasion combines credibility (ethos: why trust the speaker), logic and evidence (logos: why it is true) and meaning (pathos: why it matters to the audience)—honestly applied.

**Why.** Pure logic fails when the audience does not care or does not trust the source; pure emotion fails under scrutiny. Effective arguments establish relevant credibility, present evidence and reasoning, and connect to the audience's values and goals. Addressing the strongest counterargument openly increases credibility more than ignoring it.

**Signals.** Arguments with evidence but no connection to what the audience values; emotional appeals without substance; counterarguments ignored.

**Ask.** Why should this audience trust, believe and care about this message?

**Traps.** Manipulative emotional appeals that damage trust when noticed.

## Stories make ideas memorable {#storytelling}
<!-- terms: story storytelling narrative example anecdote case character conflict resolution hook -->

**Principle.** Illustrate important points with a concrete story—a person, a problem, a turning point, an outcome—rather than only abstract claims.

**Why.** People remember stories far better than statistics, and stories carry context that makes numbers meaningful. A good business story follows a simple arc: a situation, a complication, a resolution, and what it means. Pairing a story with data gives both memorability and credibility.

**Signals.** Presentations of abstract points without examples; case studies without a protagonist or conflict.

**Ask.** What concrete example or story would make this point stick?

**Traps.** Anecdotes presented as proof of general claims.

## Deliver feedback and hard news with care {#difficult}
<!-- terms: feedback criticism bad news incident apology mistake delay disagreement tone blame empathy -->

**Principle.** Give feedback specifically and about work, not people; deliver bad news early, plainly, with what is being done about it; apologize without deflecting.

**Why.** Vague feedback ("this needs work") is useless; specific observations with impact and a suggestion are actionable. Bad news delayed becomes worse news and damages trust. Incident communications should state what happened, who is affected, what is being done and when the next update will come. Genuine apologies take responsibility without excessive self-flagellation or excuses.

**Signals.** Criticism of people rather than work; delays disclosed at the deadline; apologies that blame others or circumstances.

**Ask.** Is this feedback specific and actionable, and is bad news being shared early with a plan?

**Traps.** Softening so much that the message is missed.

## Communicate across cultures and languages {#cross-cultural}
<!-- terms: cross-cultural international translation localization non-native english idioms time zones directness formality -->

**Principle.** For international audiences, avoid idioms and culture-specific references, spell out dates and time zones, and adapt directness and formality to cultural norms.

**Why.** Idioms and humor often fail in translation or for non-native readers. Date formats (03/04) and unqualified times create real errors. Cultures differ in directness: blunt feedback reads as rude in some cultures, while indirect hints go unnoticed in others. Simple, literal language serves both translation tools and human readers.

**Signals.** Idioms and sports metaphors in international communication; ambiguous date formats; times without zones.

**Ask.** Would a non-native reader in another time zone understand this exactly as intended?

**Traps.** Stereotyping cultures instead of learning individual preferences.
